The Only Music Theory You Need to Write a Song

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

Music theory gets talked about like a gate you have to get through before you're allowed to write anything good. It isn't. Nobody sat down and invented the rules first — people wrote music, other people noticed what kept working, and theory is the notes they took afterwards.

So it's descriptive, not prescriptive. Which means you can take the handful of bits that help you finish songs and leave the rest. Here's the handful.

1. A key is just a shortlist

Twelve notes exist. A key picks seven of them and tells you they'll sound fine together. That's genuinely the whole idea, and it's the single most useful thing theory does for a songwriter — it turns a blank page into a short menu.

A major scale is built by stacking whole steps and half steps in one fixed order: W W H W W W H. Start on C and follow that pattern and you land on the white keys, which is why C major is the one everyone learns first.

Start that same pattern somewhere else and you get a different key with the same shape. The pattern is the thing, not the letters.

Then you build a chord on each of those seven notes, and the key hands you seven chords. Their qualities are always in the same order, in every major key:

Learn that row and you've learned every major key at once. In G you'd get G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F♯° — different letters, same pattern.

One bonus: the minor key hiding inside this one is built on the 6th note. In C major that's A, so A minor uses exactly the same seven notes. Same material, different centre of gravity — which is why swapping which chord you start and end on can change the whole mood without changing a single note.

2. Major or minor comes down to one note

A basic chord is three notes: the root, something in the middle, and the fifth. Only the middle one decides whether it's major or minor.

Four semitones above the root gives you a major chord. Three gives you minor. That's it, that's the difference.

People will tell you major is happy and minor is sad, which is roughly true and often useless — tempo, lyrics and delivery do just as much work. Plenty of cheerful songs are in minor keys. But if a line feels emotionally off and you can't say why, flipping one chord between major and minor takes ten seconds and is often the fix.

3. Songs go: home, away, tension, home

Chords aren't just a pile of options — they behave differently depending on where they sit in the key. The useful version for songwriters is that chords do one of three jobs.

The I chord is home. Chords like IV and ii take you somewhere else. V is the one that makes you want to go back — it contains the note one semitone below the root of the key, and that note pulls upward hard. Your ear hears it as unfinished.

That pull is the engine behind nearly every structural trick in songwriting. Delay the resolution and you build tension, which is exactly what a pre-chorus is doing. Give it to them and the section settles.

Worth knowing: in a natural minor key that note sits a whole step below instead of a half step, so the pull is much weaker. That's part of why minor keys can feel like they drift rather than arrive.

4. Four ways to land

Phrases end, and there are basically four ways to do it. Think of them as punctuation.

  • V → I — the full stop. Sounds finished, maybe too finished.
  • IV → I — a softer landing. The "amen" ending.
  • anything → V — ends on the question. The line needs an answer, so the listener waits.
  • V → vi — sets up home and goes somewhere else instead. Useful for keeping a song alive past the point it could have ended.

If your verse feels like it stops dead before the chorus, you're probably landing on I when you want to be hanging on V.

5. About those four chords

You've heard that four chords account for most of pop music. The real numbers are more interesting than the joke.

Hooktheory has a database of roughly 83,000 song transcriptions. I–V–vi–IV really is the single most common progression in it — around 1,385 songs. But that's under 2% of the database, not 90% of music. The viral version of this claim is built on playing only the four-chord sections of songs that have other sections.

What's actually useful: that loop and its rotations — vi–IV–I–V, IV–I–V–vi — are the same four chords entered at different points, and they feel different depending on where you start. Beginning on vi sounds melancholy; beginning on I sounds open. Same chords.

So it's a reliable starting point, not a formula and not a cheat. Start there, then spend your actual effort on the melody and the words, which is where songs are won anyway.

6. Melodies land on chord tones and pass through the rest

This is the one that'll improve your melodies today.

While a chord is playing, the notes inside that chord sound settled when your melody lands on them. Everything else sounds like motion — fine in passing, unstable if you sit on it.

So: land on chord tones at the ends of phrases and on the strong beats, and use the other notes to get between them. If a melody sounds vaguely wrong over a chord you like, the fix is usually moving one long note, not rewriting the line.

It's a default, not a law. Deliberately holding a non-chord tone over a strong beat is a real technique — it creates tension you then resolve, and it's where a lot of the ache in a good melody comes from. But you want to be doing it on purpose.

7. Repetition is the structure

This gets called "form," which makes it sound grander than it is. A song is a few ideas that come back in an order the listener can follow.

The verse moves the story along and changes each time. The pre-chorus builds tension and hands over to the chorus. The chorus carries the main idea, usually with the title in the first or last line. The bridge shows up after the second chorus to break the pattern before it goes stale, so the final chorus hits like it's new.

When something you've written feels shapeless, the chords are almost never the problem. Nothing has come back yet.

8. The circle of fifths, and what it's actually for

It looks like homework. It's really just a map of which keys are neighbours.

Keys next to each other on the circle share almost all their notes — they differ by exactly one. Keys on opposite sides share very few. That's the whole practical payload.

So when you want to change key for a final chorus, move one step clockwise and nobody falls over. When you want to borrow a chord from somewhere else to add colour, reach next door. And the relative minor sitting inside each major is the same seven notes with a darker centre.

What you can skip

You don't need to read standard notation to write songs. You don't need modes, and you definitely don't need to identify Lydian by ear at a party. Counterpoint, figured bass, naming every interval on sight, having opinions about the Neapolitan sixth — all genuinely interesting, none of it between you and a finished song.

Pick it up later if you get curious. Curiosity is a much better reason than guilt.

The whole thing on one page

  1. A key is seven notes and seven chords that fit together.
  2. Major or minor is one note in the middle of the chord.
  3. Songs move home → away → tension → home.
  4. V pulls back to I. Withholding it is a tool.
  5. I–V–vi–IV is the most common loop, not a law.
  6. Land melodies on chord tones, pass through the rest.
  7. Repetition is what makes it a song.
  8. Neighbouring keys on the circle share all but one note.

All of this is in Jam Room while you're writing, which is the only time it's useful. Learn these eight, use them when you're stuck, ignore them when it's going well, and break them the second a song asks you to.
