The Only Music Theory You Need to Write a Song

November 4, 2026 · 6 min read

Songwriters tend to hold one of two beliefs about music theory, and both are wrong.

The first is that you need years of it before you're allowed to write anything serious. The second is that it'll sand the weirdness out of your songs and leave you writing competent furniture music.

The confusion comes from the word. "Theory" sounds like a rulebook handed down in advance. It isn't. It's a set of notes people took, afterwards, about what happened to sound good — closer to a field guide than a legal code. You can read a field guide without joining the birds.

Here is, genuinely, almost all of it that matters while you're writing.

1. A key is just a shortlist

Pick a key and you've been handed seven notes and seven chords that are guaranteed to work together. In C major: the notes C, D, E, F, G, A, B, and the chords C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B°.

That's the single most useful idea in theory, and it's basically an administrative convenience. It turns "any of twelve notes in any combination" into "these seven" — which is the difference between a blank page and a starting point.

2. Major and minor come down to one note

The gap between a C major chord and a C minor chord is one note moving down by a single fret. That note is the third, and it is carrying essentially the entire emotional payload while the other two sit there contributing nothing.

Major reads open and resolved. Minor reads heavy and inward. Neither is actually happy or sad on its own — tempo and lyrics do at least as much work, which is why you can write a devastating song in a major key and a cheerful one in minor. But if a song feels emotionally wrong and you can't say why, flip a major chord to minor and listen again. It's thirty seconds.

3. Four chords will get you through most songs

Inside your key, four chords do the heavy lifting: I, IV, V and vi. In C major that's C, F, G and Am.

A genuinely uncomfortable proportion of popular music is those four in some order. This gets treated as an indictment of modern songwriting roughly once a year by someone who has rediscovered it. It isn't. The combination reliably produces leaving and coming back, which is most of what a song is. If you want to finish something this week, start there and spend your creative budget on the melody and the words.

4. Tension wants resolution

Some chords are questions and some are answers. The V chord — G in the key of C — is the most obvious question in Western music. Play it and your ear leans hard toward home, like a sentence that hasn't got to the verb yet.

Nearly every structural trick in songwriting is built on this. Delay the resolution and you build anticipation, which is precisely what a pre-chorus is for. Resolve early and the section settles. Refuse to resolve at all and the song stays restless — sometimes a mistake, sometimes the entire point.

You don't need to know why V pulls toward I. You need to know that it does, and that withholding it is a tool.

5. Repetition is what makes it a song

This gets called "form," which makes it sound more impressive than it is. A song is a small number of ideas that come back.

The chorus is the thing that repeats. The verse is what changes around it. The bridge exists to break the pattern just before it wears out its welcome, so the last chorus hits harder than it has any right to. That's structure — not a formula to obey, but an explanation of why a listener can follow a song they've never heard before.

When something you've written feels shapeless, the chords are almost never the problem. Nothing has come back yet.

6. Melody sits against chords, not on top of them

This is the one that will actually improve your melodies this afternoon.

The notes inside the chord you're currently playing — the chord tones — sound settled when your melody lands on them. Every other note sounds like movement. So land on chord tones at the ends of phrases and on strong beats, and pass through the rest on your way there.

That's the whole technique. If a melody sounds vaguely wrong over a chord you like, the fix is usually moving one long note onto a chord tone — not rewriting the line, and definitely not abandoning the song and going to make tea.

What you're allowed to ignore

To write songs you do not need to read standard notation. You don't need modes, and you certainly don't need to identify Lydian by ear at a party. You don't need counterpoint, figured bass, the ability to name every interval on sight, or any opinion whatsoever about the Neapolitan sixth.

All of it is interesting. Some of it will be useful eventually, if you get curious. None of it is standing between you and a finished song, and anyone implying otherwise is selling a course.

Six ideas, one page

  1. A key gives you seven chords that fit together.
  2. Major vs. minor is one note, and it carries the mood.
  3. I, IV, V, vi will carry most songs.
  4. V pulls toward I — use it, or withhold it deliberately.
  5. Repetition is structure.
  6. Land melodies on chord tones; pass through everything else.

Theory where you're actually writing

Theory feels abstract mostly because it lives somewhere else — in a book, or in a browser tab you closed two days ago. In Jam Room the key, the chords belonging to it and the circle of fifths sit beside the lyrics you're working on, so you can check something in two seconds and get back to the line you were halfway through.

Learn these six. Use them when you're stuck, ignore them when it's going well, and break them the moment a song asks you to.
