8 Songwriting Exercises to Beat Writer's Block

November 17, 2026 · 10 min read

Nobody writes a song out of nothing. The writers who look like they do are usually running a routine they've done so often they've stopped noticing it.

These are eight of those routines — the ones that keep turning up on songwriting blogs, in Berklee's exercises, and in threads where writers describe what they actually do when nothing's coming.

Here's what each one does, and when to reach for it.

All eight are built into Jam Room.

1. Freewriting

Set a timer, write until it goes off, and don't stop for anything. No deleting, no fixing spelling, no deciding whether it's any good.

Ten minutes is the length nearly everyone lands on. The rule people stress hardest is the no-editing one, because judging and generating are two different jobs and doing both at once means doing neither. Andrea Stolpe's version drops the clock and asks for two pages instead — same idea, different fence.

The step beginners skip is the harvest. What you've written isn't a draft, it's ore. Come back the next day, read it cold, and circle only the phrases that make you look twice. Two usable lines out of ten minutes is a good session.

You'll see it claimed that this "taps your subconscious." Maybe. The part that's plainly true is less mystical: you can't edit something that doesn't exist yet, and this gets something to exist.

In Jam Room the countdown floats over your lyrics while you write, so the clock does the discipline instead of you.

2. Song Map

Before chasing words, answer a few plain questions about what the song actually is. Who's talking? Who are they talking to, and why? What's different by the end?

The most useful distinction here is between a topic and a tension. A topic is broad — heartbreak, leaving home, jealousy. The song only gets interesting when you pick the specific tension inside it. "Missing someone" is a topic. "I deleted their number but still know it by heart" is a song.

There's a good test floating around for whether you've found it: you should be able to name the emotion in one word and the subject in a short phrase. If it takes you a paragraph, it isn't focused yet.

What this prevents is the common failure of a page of nice lines that add up to nothing. Once there's a target, a line stops being judged on whether it's pretty and starts being judged on whether it gets you closer. The other thing it catches early is point-of-view drift — songs where the "you" quietly changes identity halfway through and the listener stops being able to follow.

Rough answers are fine. You're sketching a direction, not signing anything.

3. Wildcard

You're handed things you didn't choose — an object, a place, a point of view, and one rule you have to keep — and you write inside them against a clock.

The argument everyone makes for this is that too much freedom is the actual problem. The best phrasing of it I came across: limitations don't limit you, they limit distraction. A blank page offers every option at once, which is precisely why it's paralysing.

The rules that work are the ones that cost you something. "No rhymes." "Only one-syllable words." "Every line starts with I." "Write only questions." If a constraint doesn't make you uncomfortable it isn't doing anything — the discomfort is the signal it's working. Writing from an object's point of view is a good one for this, because an object notices things a human narrator would walk straight past.

This is also one of the few exercises here with proper research behind it. A 2024 study in the Journal of Management Inquiry followed professional pop songwriting teams at work and found they don't just tolerate constraints, they actively set, break and reset them as a working method. Broader reviews of creativity research agree, with one catch worth knowing: the relationship is an inverted U. A few constraints help, a pile of them stifles, and they work better early than late.

So: four of them, at the start. Not forty, and not when you're nearly done.

Jam Room deals the four and lets you reshuffle. Keep going until the combination feels slightly impossible, then write it anyway.

4. Check-In

Before writing anything, mark where you actually are — a point on a scale from very unpleasant to very pleasant, the words that fit it — and then answer a few honest questions. Not about the song. About you.

The case for this is practical rather than therapeutic: you can't write something true if you haven't got any true material to hand. As one songwriting teacher puts it, it's easier to write something you feel than something you've made up.

Sentence stems work better than being told to "write honestly." The ones that come up most: I remember… / What I could not say was… / The part that still stays with me is… / If this feeling had one line, it would say… Then find the line that feels most alive and leave the rest.

Two warnings worth passing on. Pick one feeling or one memory, not your whole life story — the aim is honest, not complete. And self-censorship is the thing that ruins it; if you're writing the version you'd be comfortable showing someone, you're not doing the exercise.

There's a real finding behind the naming part: putting a feeling into words measurably changes how your brain processes it — labelling an emotion increases prefrontal activity and dampens the amygdala response. What that supports is narrow but useful. A vague feeling can't be written about. A named one can.

You'll also see claims that journaling improves your physical health. That literature is much shakier than the posts citing it suggest, so take the writing benefit and leave the rest.

5. Songwriting Prompt

Shuffle through "write a song about…" ideas until one pulls at you, then write straight at it.

Prompts don't supply inspiration. They remove the choosing problem, which is usually what's actually stopping you. Every prompt list says some version of "this isn't cheating" — and they're right, but the more useful framing I found was blunter: bad songs are reps. You're not trying to produce a good song from the prompt, you're trying to produce a song, often.

Ten minutes, no editing, and pick a perspective before you start — first person, second person, or a character who isn't you. The goal is momentum.

One oddity: almost every prompt list hands you fifty ideas and says nothing about how to choose. That shuffle-until-something-tugs step matters more than the list does. The tug is you finding out what you already wanted to write about.

6. Sense Sprint

Take something small and ordinary — a cold cup of coffee, a knock at 3am — and describe it one sense at a time for ten minutes. Stop dead when the timer goes, even mid-sentence.

This is the exercise most often prescribed for the single most common weakness in lyrics: staying abstract. Words like "love" and "loneliness" are too big to land. The exercise trains you to build a library of images instead, and over time you stop reaching for the abstract word first.

It's seven senses, not five. Alongside the obvious ones there's the organic sense — what's happening inside your body, pulse, breath, tension — and the kinesthetic sense, your body's relationship to the space around it. Those two are usually where the writing stops being generic.

Three tips that recur. Start with your weakest senses — smell, taste, touch — because they reach different memories than sight does. Afterwards, audit which senses you ignored entirely; that's your blind spot. And keep pushing past the first description: "the kitchen smelled like dinner cooking" is where most people stop, and it's nothing. "Cold" is nothing. A skin on the surface and a ring where the mug sat is a song.

The mistake to avoid is writing lyrics. This is prose. No rhyme, no meter, no trying to be good.

And there's a genuinely interesting piece of science here. A 2012 study found that textural metaphors — "she'd had a rough day" — activate the touch-processing part of the brain, while literal paraphrases meaning exactly the same thing don't. Sensory language isn't just more vivid as a figure of speech. It's processed differently.

Jam Room hands you the object so you don't lose the first two minutes picking one, and lets you drop any sense that isn't pulling its weight.

7. Word Ladder

Pick a trade — a sailor, a surgeon, a beekeeper — and raid its vocabulary for verbs. Then grab ten nouns from whatever's in front of you. Then start connecting pairs that have no business together.

The point is that verbs and nouns get stuck to each other. Hearts break, rain falls, time passes. The exercise prises them apart so you can put them back together wrong, which is where phrases like "rusty lips" come from — things your editing brain would have vetoed before you ever saw them.

Go fast and don't curate. The lists aren't the point and most pairings will be nonsense. Give the pairing step about two minutes, then see which ones won't leave you alone.

Everyone who writes about this stresses the same thing: it isn't for generating finished lyrics. Jeff Tweedy, whose version of it is the one most people have met, says he uses it less to produce words than to remind himself how much fun they are when he isn't judging himself. Treat the output as a warm-up, not a verse.

Related, and genuinely good trivia: Bowie liked the cut-up version of this so much that he and Brian Eno built software to do it, called the Verbasizer.

8. Three Random Words

Three unrelated words. All three have to end up in the song.

The reason to bother is that three fixed points force moves you wouldn't otherwise make. A blank page lets you drift back to your defaults; three words you didn't pick won't let you.

The mistake everyone makes first is taking the words literally. "Rain" does not have to mean weather — it can be loneliness, or relief, or the thing that happened the day everything changed. Let the word mean something to you before you try to use it. The fastest way in is to attach each one to a specific memory, person or place rather than to a concept.

Useful variants: give each word a job — one for the verse, one for the chorus, one for the bridge. Or make them an object, an action and an emotion. Adding a short time limit on top of the words works better than you'd expect, because it stops you trying to be clever about the connections.

In Jam Room each word ticks off as it turns up in your draft, which makes it a small puzzle rather than three things to keep remembering.

Which one to reach for

They're not interchangeable. Roughly:

  • Nothing to write about — Check-In, or a Prompt.
  • Something to write about, nothing on the page — Freewriting.
  • Plenty on the page, no shape — Song Map.
  • Lyrics that stay vague — Sense Sprint.
  • Everything sounds like your last song — Wildcard or Word Ladder.
  • Stuck halfway through a draft — Three Random Words.

All eight are in Jam Room with the timers, shuffles and prompts already set up, and whatever comes out saves into the song you're working on. But the exercises themselves are old, free and belong to everyone — so if you've got a kitchen timer and ten minutes, start there.

Pick the one that matches what's actually wrong, and go write something bad.
