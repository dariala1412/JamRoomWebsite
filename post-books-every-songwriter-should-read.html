10 Books That Will Make You a Better Songwriter

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

There's no shortage of "best songwriting books" lists out there, and they all disagree with each other. So instead of just picking our favorites, we pulled together the titles that keep showing up across a bunch of those lists, plus what Berklee actually assigns and what working songwriters recommend.

Ten books came out of it. Some are workbooks with exercises, some are closer to music theory, and a couple aren't really about songwriting at all — they're just good to read in between writing sessions. There's something here whether you're just starting out or you've been doing this a while.

1. How to Write One SongJeff Tweedy

Beginner

A short one, aimed at people who've never finished a song. The idea is that writing one song is something anyone can do, which is a lot less scary than trying to become a songwriter.

Tweedy is encouraging and pretty funny, and a good chunk of the book is him talking you out of your own head. There are practical exercises for when you get stuck. Readers keep saying it helped them quiet the inner critic, and a lot of them mention it's worth reading even if you never write a note.

It's just a matter of telling yourself that your creation is OK, no matter what it is.

— Jeff Tweedy, How to Write One Song
Find it on Amazon →

2. Writing Better LyricsPat Pattison

Beginner → Intermediate

The lyric book everyone recommends, and it's been that way for about twenty years. Pattison teaches at Berklee and the book works like a course — around fifty exercises, with examples pulled from twenty-odd chart-topping songs.

The famous part is object writing: you set a timer and write about one object through your senses. His students consistently say it's the single thing that changed their writing most. There's also good material on metaphor, dodging clichés and playing with point of view. Works whether you're new or have been at it for years.

Both of these are in Jam Room. Sense Sprint hands you an everyday object and walks you through it one sense at a time, and Wildcard deals you an object, a place, a point of view and one rule you have to keep.

You can't tell unless you show first. Showing makes the telling more powerful because your senses and your mind are both engaged.

— Pat Pattison, Writing Better Lyrics
Find it on Thalia →

3. Popular Lyric WritingAndrea Stolpe

Intermediate

A ten-step guide to writing lyrics that actually tell a story. It's a set text at Berklee, Belmont and USC, so it's basically a course in book form.

The big technique is toggling — you alternate concrete, sense-based lines with lines about feeling, and each section of a song gets its own pattern. Stolpe pulls apart hit lyrics by people like John Mayer and Faith Hill to show it working. Good if you want a repeatable method rather than waiting for inspiration.

Find it on Amazon →

4. The Craft of SongwritingScarlet Keys

Advanced

The deep one on the music itself — melody, harmony, lyrics and form, and how each choice changes the way a song feels. Keys teaches at Berklee, and it comes with audio tracks so you can hear what every technique actually does.

It's a small book that people end up keeping on the desk as a reference. The chapter on chord substitutions gets singled out constantly — plenty of reviewers say it's worth the price on its own. Best once you already know your way around a song.

Handy to have something to try it on. Jam Room's chord catalog has the circle of fifths and the diatonic chords for any key, the melody panel gives you piano, guitar and scale pads with scales and modes, and it'll work out the chords or the melody from you playing or humming.

Find it on Amazon →

5. Switched On PopNate Sloan & Charlie Harding

Beginner → Intermediate

From the podcast. Sixteen chapters, each one taking apart a famous song to explain a single idea — meter, counterpoint, sampling, sound design, rhyme, and so on.

It's genuinely fun to read, and the illustrations do a lot of the work: reverb drawn as a bouncing ball, song forms drawn as rollercoasters. The songs run from Britney Spears and Outkast to Kendrick Lamar and Carly Rae Jepsen. Great if you want to understand how records work without sitting through a theory class.

On the rhyme side, Jam Room will give you rhymes for whatever word your cursor is sitting on.

Find it on Amazon →

6. Song BuildingMarty Dodson & Bill O'Hanlon

Intermediate

A practical little book about building songs instead of waiting around for inspiration. It's 118 pages and full of exercises, so you can work through it fast.

The main idea is blueprinting — sketching the structure before you write, which sounds limiting but mostly stops you stalling halfway. It's especially good on second verses and on making co-writes go smoother. Dodson's written six number ones across country, pop, bluegrass, Christian and K-pop, and the advice stays plain and usable throughout.

Blueprinting is more or less what Song Map does in Jam Room — a few plain questions to find the spine of the song, like who's talking and what's at stake. Sections come with tags (Intro, Verse, Pre-Chorus, Chorus, Hook, Bridge, Solo) so you can lay the whole shape out first.

Find it on Amazon →

7. The Philosophy of Modern SongBob Dylan

Advanced

Sixty-six short essays, all about other people's songs — Webb Pierce to The Clash, mostly stuff from the fifties through the seventies. It's a big illustrated book full of photographs, and it reads like wandering round a record shop with someone very opinionated.

Dylan writes in a punchy, hard-boiled voice and keeps slipping into second person, so you end up inside the song rather than reading about it. No method, no exercises. What you get is someone noticing exactly how a record works — the arrangement, the performance, why it lands. David Remnick called it rich, riffy and funny, which is about right.

Find it on Amazon →

8. The Creative Act: A Way of BeingRick Rubin

Any level

Seventy-eight short chapters Rubin calls Areas of Thought — Seeds, Beginner's Mind, Self-Doubt, The Source. They aren't numbered, so you can open it anywhere and read a page.

It's about being someone who makes things rather than about songwriting specifically, and it reads almost like a book of proverbs. People reach for it most when they're in a dry spell — it's very good at quieting the voice that judges everything before it exists. The audiobook, read by Rubin himself, is the version a lot of people prefer.

Jam Room has a few things pointed at exactly this: Check-In asks you a few honest questions before you start, and Three Random Words and Songwriting Prompt both exist to get something down before you've decided whether it's any good.

Ultimately, your desire to create must be greater than your fear of it.

— Rick Rubin, The Creative Act
Find it on Thalia →

9. Steal Like an ArtistAustin Kleon

Any level

A tiny illustrated book built around ten ideas, starting with "steal like an artist" and ending with "creativity is subtraction." You can read the whole thing in half an hour.

The core point is that nothing is original and everyone is shaped by what they love, so copy your heroes until your own thing shows up. Some of it will just confirm what you're already doing, and some of it will have you reaching for a notebook straight away. Flip through it in thirty minutes, stay inspired for weeks — that's the usual experience.

Every artist gets asked the question, 'Where do you get your ideas?' The honest artist answers, 'I steal them.'

— Austin Kleon, Steal Like an Artist
Find it on Thalia →

10. The Future of SongwritingKristin Hersh

Advanced

A short one, written as a series of conversations between Hersh and a comedian friend while they were both playing a festival in Sydney over Christmas.

She's thinking out loud about what streaming and money have done to music, and about what songs are actually for. It circles the subject rather than arguing its way to a conclusion, which suits it. Wry, warm and a bit mystical — the Irish Times called it a TED talk from your most witchy friend. A good one to read after the craft books.

Find it on Amazon →

One caveat

It's easy to turn "reading about songwriting" into its own hobby, separate from actually writing songs. A few chapters a week feels like progress even when nothing's been written.

The books that work best here are the ones that hand you something to do, not just something to think about. So pick whichever one looks good, and try to get through an exercise or two in the same week you read it — before it turns into another book on the shelf.

Read a chapter, then go write something bad.
