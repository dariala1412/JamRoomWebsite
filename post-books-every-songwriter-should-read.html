12 Books Worth a Songwriter's Time

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

There's no shortage of "best songwriting books" lists out there, and most of them disagree with each other. So this one is built differently: these are the titles that actually keep turning up across a stack of those lists. Three of them appear on nearly all of them. After that it thins out fast.

Twelve of them, in the order it made sense to read them.

1. Writing Better LyricsPat Pattison

On every list I looked at, usually first, and it deserves it. Pattison taught at Berklee for decades and the book is mostly exercises rather than explanation. Object writing, sense writing — you set a timer and describe something using all your senses, and you do it every day until something shifts.

It's work, though. If you don't do the exercises it's a waste of money. I did maybe half of them, and the half I did changed how I write. So. Do more than half.

You can't tell unless you show first. Showing makes the telling more powerful because your senses and your mind are both engaged.

— Pat Pattison, Writing Better Lyrics
Find it on Amazon →

2. Songwriters on SongwritingPaul Zollo

Interviews. Dozens of them, and they're long ones — Zollo doesn't do the thing where every question is secretly about the new record.

The useful part is that nobody agrees with anybody. One writer swears you always start with the lyric, the next thinks that's lunacy. You come away realising there's no correct method, which is either liberating or deeply annoying depending on what you wanted the book to tell you.

I have somehow come to believe that the only lyrics that I'm really interested in are the lyrics that I find, not the lyrics I invent.

— Paul Simon, interviewed in Songwriters on Songwriting
Find it on Amazon →

3. How to Write One SongJeff Tweedy

This one actually is about songwriting, technically, but it reads more like permission than instruction. Tweedy's whole move is shrinking the question from "how do I become a songwriter" down to "how do I write one song," which turns out to matter more than it sounds like it should.

It's just a matter of telling yourself that your creation is OK, no matter what it is.

— Jeff Tweedy, How to Write One Song
Find it on Amazon →

4. TunesmithJimmy Webb

Webb wrote "Wichita Lineman" and "MacArthur Park" and "By The Time I Get To Phoenix," so he's earned the right to be opinionated, and he absolutely is.

It's a thick book and it swings between things. Some chapters are properly technical, with real music theory in them. Others are just him telling stories about the business in the sixties and seventies, which I enjoyed more than I expected to. Parts of it are dated. Doesn't really matter.

Songwriting is Hell on Earth. If it isn't, then you're doing it wrong.

— Jimmy Webb, Tunesmith
Find it on Amazon →

5. Bird by BirdAnne Lamott

About writing novels, not songs, and I don't care about that either. It's funny, it's honest about how bad the early stage of anything feels, and the chapter on "shitty first drafts" alone has probably unstuck more songs than most of the actual songwriting books on this page.

Bird by bird, buddy. Just take it bird by bird.

— Anne Lamott, Bird by Bird
Find it on Amazon →

6. Melody in SongwritingJack Perricone

Another Berklee one. This is the book people point at when someone asks how you actually write a tune, as opposed to writing words and chords and hoping a melody turns up.

Fair warning: it's the most textbook-ish thing here. There's notation. If you don't read music you'll still get something out of it, but you'll be working harder than everyone else in the room.

The best commercial songs have an appeal to the masses because they contain an innate truth, a connection that cannot be denied.

— Jack Perricone, Melody in Songwriting
Find it on Amazon →

7. The Creative Act: A Way of BeingRick Rubin

No lyric advice, no chords, nothing you could point to and call a technique — this is a book about creativity in general, not songwriting specifically, and it would apply just as well to someone who paints. Short meditations, no instructions, occasionally maddening. I know people who find it profound and people who think it's 400 pages of a very rich man being vague at you. I land somewhere in the middle — there are ten pages in here that rearranged how I think about writing, buried inside a lot of pages that didn't.

Ultimately, your desire to create must be greater than your fear of it.

— Rick Rubin, The Creative Act
Find it on Amazon →

8. The Addiction FormulaFriedemann Findeisen

This one surprised me by how often it came up. It's self-published and it's about hooks — why certain songs lodge in your head and won't leave.

Much more "here's a technique, here's a song that uses it" than the others. Some people find it cynical, and fair enough, it's a book about engineering catchiness. I'd say read it after you've got a few finished songs, not before.

Find it on Amazon →

9. The War of ArtSteven Pressfield

Short, and it runs on one idea stretched across the whole book — a force he calls "Resistance" that shows up whenever you're about to do something that matters to you. By page 140 you've got it, possibly by page 60, but the first third is genuinely good at naming the thing that keeps you from opening the file.

Resistance has no strength of its own. Every ounce of juice it possesses comes from us.

— Steven Pressfield, The War of Art
Find it on Amazon →

10. Six Steps to Songwriting SuccessJason Blume

The business one. Blume's had actual cuts with major-label artists, and roughly half the book is about the part nobody teaches — demos, pitching, publishers, what a songplugger does all day.

If you only write for yourself you can skip this entirely and lose nothing. If you want other people to record your songs, it's the most practical thing on the list.

The thing I want to stress the most about melody writing is melody re-writing.

— Jason Blume, Six Steps to Songwriting Success
Find it on Amazon →

11. The Craft of Lyric WritingSheila Davis

Old. 1985 old. The examples are songs your parents liked, and it reads like a textbook from a time when people were fine with that.

But it's still the clearest explanation of song form I've come across — AABA versus verse-chorus, where a title belongs, why a hook lands where it lands. Forty years and nobody's really replaced it, which is a bit embarrassing for everyone who's written a songwriting book since.

Don't fall prey to the ‘genius’ syndrome and outsmart yourself by being too complicated.

— Sheila Davis, The Craft of Lyric Writing
Find it on Amazon →

12. Steal Like an ArtistAustin Kleon

Thin, cheap, more of a long pamphlet than a book, and I mean that as a compliment — you'll finish it on one train ride. The whole thesis is that nothing is original and pretending otherwise is just stage fright with extra steps, which is exactly the thing to hand someone who's convinced their chorus sounds too much like somebody else's.

Every artist gets asked the question, 'Where do you get your ideas?' The honest artist answers, 'I steal them.'

— Austin Kleon, Steal Like an Artist
Find it on Amazon →

If you're only buying one

Pattison. It's not close.

Two: Pattison and Zollo, because one is pure craft and the other is forty people telling you there's no such thing as craft.

If you've been writing a while and feel stuck inside your own habits, Tunesmith or the Davis. If you want somebody else to cut your song, Blume. And if the actual problem is that you haven't written anything in three weeks, skip straight to Lamott or Pressfield — none of the craft books will help with that part.

The obvious problem with all of this

Reading about songwriting is an excellent way to feel productive while writing nothing. I say that as someone with a shelf.

So whatever you pick, try to get the exercises off the page the same week. Jam Room has some of this built in — timed freewriting, sense-based prompts, that sort of thing — mostly because I kept reading about them and then not doing them.

Read a chapter, then go write something bad.
