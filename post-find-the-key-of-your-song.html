How to Find the Key of a Song You Already Wrote

November 4, 2026 · 5 min read

Nobody sits down and decides on a key. What actually happens is your hands land somewhere, the shape sounds good, and twenty minutes vanish. By the time you surface you've got most of a song and absolutely no idea what to call any of it.

Then a bandmate asks what key it's in and you say "uh, guitar key" and send them a voice memo instead.

Here's how to work it out backwards, using nothing but the chords already sitting in front of you.

Why bother at all

Key isn't trivia you memorise to feel legitimate. It's practical in four specific ways: it tells you which chords are still on the table when the bridge needs one more, it lets you move the whole song down a tone because the chorus sits just above your range, it means a bandmate can learn the thing in ten seconds, and it turns the capo from a guessing game into a decision.

That's it. If none of those come up, you can genuinely skip this.

Method 1: List the chords, find the family

Write down every chord in the song, ignoring repeats. Say you end up with:

G  ·  Em  ·  C  ·  D

Now find the key whose seven chords contain all of them. Every major key follows the same pattern — major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished — so G major gives you:

G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F♯°

All four of yours are in there. You're in G major. Congratulations, you have been in G major this entire time.

Try C major instead (C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B°) and your D stops fitting — your song has D major, C major wants D minor. That mismatch is the whole tell. One wrong chord type and the key is eliminated.

In practice this takes one or two guesses, not twelve. Start by assuming your first or last chord is the key and check whether everything else falls in line. It usually does.

Method 2: Find the chord that feels like home

Play the progression and stop in different places. One chord will feel like an ending — like you could walk away and the song would be finished. The others will feel like they're leaning somewhere, waiting for you to get on with it.

That resting chord is almost always the key. It's the gravity everything else is orbiting.

If you can hum the song and land on a note that feels like "done," you've found the key. Your ear got there first; the theory is just paperwork.

This is faster than method 1 and weirdly reliable. The catch is that it works badly on songs that deliberately refuse to resolve — which is a real technique, and the reason some songs feel permanently unsettled in a way you can't put your finger on.

Method 3: Look at the last chord, with suspicion

Songs tend to end on home. If yours finishes on F, you're probably in F major. Or D minor. We'll get to that.

The suspicion is warranted because plenty of songs end on a hanging chord specifically to avoid closure, or just fade out mid-progression because that's what the demo did. Treat the last chord as a strong hint and confirm it with method 1.

Major or minor? Same chords, different gravity

Here's the part that trips everyone up, including people who should know better.

C major and A minor contain exactly the same seven chords. So do G major and E minor. Every major key has a relative minor that shares its entire family, which means listing your chords cannot tell them apart. No amount of staring will help.

What tells them apart is where the song rests:

  • Resolves to C → C major.
  • Resolves to Am → A minor. Same chords. Different centre of gravity.

The relative minor always sits three semitones below the major: C → Am, G → Em, D → Bm. So if your song feels considerably darker than your chord list suggests it should, you're probably sitting in the minor and haven't noticed.

When one chord refuses to cooperate

Sometimes six chords agree and one doesn't. That's usually not a mistake — it's a borrowed chord, pulled in from a parallel key because it sounded good. A ♭VII, a minor iv, a major III in a minor key: all common, all deliberate-sounding, all borrowed.

Don't let one outlier talk you out of your answer. You're still in the key the other six point to. You just took a detour, and detours are generally the most interesting bar in the song.

If several chords stop fitting partway through, that's a different diagnosis — you modulated. The song has two keys, not one wrong one, and you should probably feel quite pleased with yourself.

The two-minute version

  1. Write out every distinct chord.
  2. Guess the key from the first or last chord.
  3. Build that key's seven and check your list against it.
  4. One chord off? Borrowed. Three off? Key change.
  5. Major or minor is decided by whichever chord sounds like home.

Then write it down somewhere

The actual hard part isn't working out the key. It's still knowing it in three weeks.

Write first. Label later. But do label it.
