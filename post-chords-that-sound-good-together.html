Chords That Actually Sound Good Together

November 4, 2026 · 5 min read

You found two chords. They sounded great, so you played them eleven times in a row to make sure they weren't a fluke. Then you added a third and something went quietly wrong — not wrong wrong, just off, in a way you couldn't name. So you went back to the two that worked. Eleven more times.

That's not a taste problem. Nine times out of ten the third chord came from a different key, and your ear caught it instantly. Ears are brilliant at this. They're just terrible at explaining themselves.

Here's the explanation, and it takes about four minutes.

Every key hands you seven chords

Pick a key — C major, since it has no sharps or flats and nobody needs that today. It's built from seven notes: C, D, E, F, G, A, B. Build a chord on each one and you get seven chords:

C  ·  Dm  ·  Em  ·  F  ·  G  ·  Am  ·  B°

That's the family. Play them in any order, any combination, and the result will sound like music. Not necessarily good music — you can still write something unbearable using only these — but never wrong. Which is the whole point. You've gone from twelve notes and infinite combinations down to seven options that are guaranteed to get along.

The pattern is the same every time

This is the bit worth memorising, and it's one line. Whatever key you're in, the chord types run in this order:

  • major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished

Musicians write it as I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii° — capitals major, lowercase minor. Drop that pattern onto any starting note and the key builds itself:

  • C major: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B°
  • G major: G, Am, Bm, C, D, Em, F♯°
  • D major: D, Em, F♯m, G, A, Bm, C♯°

Learn the pattern once and you've got all twelve keys. Memorise them as twelve separate lists and you've got a long weekend ahead of you and nothing to show for it.

Four of them do nearly all the work

Of those seven, four carry an embarrassing proportion of recorded music: I, IV, V and vi. In C that's C, F, G and Am.

Line them up as I–V–vi–IV and you have the progression underneath a genuinely alarming number of pop, rock and country songs from the last fifty years. Rotate the same four so they start somewhere else — vi–IV–I–V, or IV–I–V–vi — and it feels like a different progression while using identical ingredients.

People love to point this out as though it's a scandal. It isn't. It's four chords that reliably produce departure and return, which is most of what a song needs. Spend your originality on the melody and the words, where listeners actually notice it.

If a chord sounds wrong, it usually isn't wrong. It's just visiting from another key.

You can ignore the diminished one

That seventh chord, B° in the key of C, is tense and unstable and turns up constantly in jazz and classical writing. In pop songs it is essentially a rumour. Leave it out of your life entirely and nothing bad will happen to you.

Six chords, then. Honestly, four.

Minor keys are the same family with a different home

A minor contains exactly the same seven chords as C major. Identical. The only difference is where the song comes to rest.

Start and end on C and it sounds major. Start and end on Am and those same chords sound minor. Nothing else has to change.

Which means if your song feels suspiciously upbeat for lyrics about a breakup, you have a ten-second experiment available: start on the vi instead. It's the cheapest mood swing in songwriting, and it works often enough to be slightly annoying.

Three things to try today

Write the seven down before you start. Pick a key, list its chords, and refuse to leave that list for one song. Constraint speeds everything up. When every chord is available you audition all of them; when seven are, you actually choose.

When a progression stalls, swap inside the family. The two most useful substitutions are I → vi and IV → ii. They share most of their notes, so the change is subtle — the progression doesn't break, it just gets darker around the edges.

When you want out, leave deliberately. Once the seven start feeling predictable, break the rule on purpose. A ♭VII (B♭ in the key of C) or a minor iv (Fm in C) will stand out because everything around it belongs. That's the actual difference between a wrong chord and an interesting one: you meant it, and you put it somewhere.

Keeping the family in front of you

This is why Jam Room shows the diatonic chords for whatever key your jam is in — the seven that fit, sorted into triads and sevenths, with the circle of fifths one tap away for when you want to wander off. You can also just play a chord at your phone and let it name the thing, which helps on the regular occasions when your fingers find something good before your brain catches up.

Pick a key. Stay in the family. Leave when the song asks you to.
